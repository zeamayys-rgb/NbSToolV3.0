Draw a boundary. See what the land can take.

NbS Tool is a free web tool for planning Nature-based Solutions in Southeast Asia. Give it a boundary and it reports what’s already there, what’s happening to it, and whether protecting, managing or restoring it fits. From there you can write the project up, plan the monitoring and keep the record as it runs. No GIS software needed.

Find the place.

Phase 0 · Explore the map

Five layer groups sit on the map while you look around: country-specific layers, site information, nature, climate and people. Search a province or district and its boundary is drawn as a tracing guide. It doesn’t become your project polygon.

Phase 1 · Select location

Draw a polygon, or upload a zipped shapefile, KML or KMZ. The upload is the one method you can repeat exactly, so use it for anything you’ll need to defend. Check the area on the confirm card, set the project duration, and confirm. Nothing is computed before that point.

Read the land.

Phase 2 · Site characterisation

What is here. Every layer is clipped to your boundary and summarised in four groups: general context, nature, climate and people. This phase reads and makes no recommendation. Three of its figures carry everything that follows: land cover now, forest cover ten years earlier, and the yearly rate of forest loss.

Phase 3 · Threat profile

What is happening to it. How much of each ecosystem is disturbed and what’s driving that, whether small farms, logging, roads or fire; drainage pressure on peat; and the risk of flood, fire, landslide and drought. It doesn’t pick the pathway. It decides which activities are worth switching on.

Choose the pathway.

Phase 4 · Pathway selection

Land cover now is compared with land cover ten years earlier, and both are read against the reference ecosystem: a modelled picture of what should grow there, which is the only way to tell degraded land from naturally open land. Each hectare lands on one answer, Protect, Manage, Restore or carbon ineligible, with the reason stated. The activities then follow from the ground, not the label. Restoring forest means planting; restoring mangrove means breaching pond dykes; restoring peat means raising the water table. It’s a screening answer, not a prescription.

Phase 5 · Potential benefit

The benefits those activities are expected to deliver, in three pillars, with carbon projected over the duration you set. Nothing appears here that an earlier phase didn’t select. Say it’s a carbon project and the tool also shows the deductions for leakage, uncertainty and buffer, and the net figures. Screening figures, labelled as such.

Write it up.

Turn the analysis into a Feasibility Study Document Template, with the maps, baselines, benefit assessment and socio-economic profile a feasibility study starts from, or a CCB Template laid out to the Climate, Community & Biodiversity Standards, which can be paired with Verra or Gold Standard. The tool fills in what it can. The parts only your project knows are left for you. Documents are built from a saved project, so save first.

Keep it in one place.

Every project you save sits in one dashboard: the boundary, the analysis, the documents, the monitoring plan and who you’ve shared it with. Set the privacy level, invite collaborators, import a project that’s already running, and when it’s ready, put it forward to the NbS Incubator. Saving is also what unlocks the documents and the monitoring plan.

Keep the record.

The activities you chose come with the indicators that can show whether they’re working. Keep, drop or add to them and set how often each is measured. Then record field data against the plan period by period, with photos as evidence, and export a report when someone asks for one.

How this works

Nature, people, climate. A good project answers to all three.

A Nature-based Solution protects, manages or restores an ecosystem to deal with a problem people actually have: a river that floods, a harvest that fails, a forest being cleared. The Coalition’s test is whether a project delivers for nature, people and climate at the same time, so the tool reports its results in those three columns.

NbS Tool is developed by the SCeNe Coalition, the Southeast Asia Climate and Nature-based Solutions Coalition: non-governmental organisations with a long presence in the region, working to get more good projects funded. Its mark holds three things at once, an orangutan, a face and a cloud. Nature, people and climate, inseparable.

Visit scenecoalition.org (opens in a new tab)

Nature

What’s living there and how intact it is: forest integrity, habitat for threatened species, overlap with protected areas and Key Biodiversity Areas, and whether the habitat still connects.

People

Who lives with the site: how many people, how exposed they are to flood and drought, and the official district figures on work, income, schooling and water. Tenure and consent still come from the community itself.

Climate

Carbon in the trees and in the soil, measured now and projected over the project’s life: what protecting a forest keeps out of the air, what restoring one could pull back in. Screening figures, labelled as such.

Before anyone drew a boundary, we went and listened.

SCeNe Coalition teams went to these sites to sit with the people who look after the land, before any boundary was drawn. What they heard decides how each project is scoped in NbS Tool, what its project document says, and what is put forward to the NbS Incubator. One thing held everywhere: the communities agreed to a study, not to a project. Every rule that comes later goes back to them for a decision.

Kibongcog

Bukidnon, Philippines

Kibongcog

Bukidnon, Philippines

Corn climbs the slopes the loggers left, and above it the forest closes in, still home to the Philippine Eagle. The village has declared that forest protected: good for the eagle, awkward for finance, since a forest that would stand anyway earns no carbon credit.

Men name landslides first; women name waste and broken facilities, and their maps barely show the forest at all.

In NbS Tool that becomes two cases: cleared slopes scoped for native agroforestry with the landslide layer open, and an intact forest that grants or public money, not credits, will have to carry. The feasibility document says so.

Lematang Waterfall

South Sumatra, Indonesia

Lematang Waterfall

South Sumatra, Indonesia

HaKI laid its paperwork on the table: social baselines pulled from permit files, carbon baselines lifted from provincial emission plans, and biodiversity baselines built from what villagers remember seeing. Then NbS Tool went on the table too, and we asked the room to break it.

In Semendo we ran the same test on the ground. One polygon sat four kilometres from where it belonged, and two village forests overlapped.

Those corrections come first. Nobody writes a document for the incubator on a map the village doesn’t recognise.

Teluk Pambang

Riau, Indonesia

Teluk Pambang

Riau, Indonesia

A decree has put this mangrove in the village’s hands. The women know it best: they gather shellfish and crabs in it and do most of the planting, and their day runs from before dawn to late evening, so they miss the meetings.

Most of what threatens the forest sits outside the boundary: shrimp ponds, their access roads, plantations. Few of the pond owners are from the village.

So the team draws two boundaries in NbS Tool, the village forest and the wider area around it, and the restoration target stays provisional until the tide at the old oil-palm ground is checked.

Kampong Thom

Cambodia

Kampong Thom

Cambodia

Three community forests share one catchment and one project, and little else: one hears the gibbon and prays at a lake, another counts hornbills each fruiting season.

Women have the run of forest and fields, and little say over what gets sold. Cattle would put income in their hands; grazing already threatens all three forests. Acacia pays, and acacia is invasive here.

Each forest gets its own boundary in NbS Tool, fire and drought come off the threat profile before anyone picks species, and grazing and acacia rules are agreed with the committees first.

Shaped by the people who'll use it.

Built by the SCeNe Coalition.

The Southeast Asia Climate and Nature-based Solutions Coalition is a group of conservation and research organisations that have long worked across the region. They want more money going to projects that are good for nature and fair to the people who live there, and better data is how you tell those projects apart. The tool is one of three things they’ve built, alongside a set of high-integrity criteria and the NbS Incubator.
